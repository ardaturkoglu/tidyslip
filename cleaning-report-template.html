Cleaning completion report template (with before & after photos)

A cleaning completion report tells your client what was cleaned, what it looked like before and after, and anything that needs their attention. This guide shows what to include, gives you a free template to copy, and explains how to take photos that are easy to compare.

When clients want a report

Not every client needs one. A short report is most useful when the client isn’t there to see the result:

  • Vacation rental and Airbnb turnovers. The host is often in another city and needs to know the place is ready, what supplies are low and whether anything was damaged.
  • Move-in and move-out cleans. The owner, tenant or property manager wants to see each room, and sometimes the condition you found it in.
  • Offices and recurring commercial jobs. A manager may need to show that the agreed tasks were done.
  • Deep cleans and one-off jobs. Before and after photos show the work behind the price.

What to include

1. Job header

Your business name and contact details, the client’s name, the date, and the type of clean (standard, deep, move-out, turnover). Leave out private details the report doesn’t need, such as gate or key-box codes.

2. Rooms, one section each

Use the names your client uses: “Main bathroom”, “Kitchen”, “Bedroom 2”. One section per room is easier to read than one long list.

3. A checklist per room

List the tasks and mark which ones were done. If a task was skipped, say why in a few words (“oven not included in this visit”).

4. Before and after photo pairs

Put each pair side by side, from the same angle. Two or three pairs per room are usually enough; pick the spots that changed the most.

5. Issues noticed

Damage, supplies running low, items left behind, or anything that needs maintenance. Each with a photo and one factual sentence.

6. Condition on arrival (optional)

For move-outs and turnovers, a few photos taken before you start help explain what you found.

7. A short note to the client

One or two sentences: what went well, anything you’d suggest for next time.

Before and after photo tips

  • Same spot, same angle. Stand where you stood for the before photo. Pick a corner of the counter or a door frame as a marker.
  • Same light. Turn on the same lights for both photos. Avoid shooting straight into a window.
  • One wide, one close. A wide shot shows the room; a close shot shows the detail that changed (the stovetop, the grout).
  • Keep the phone the same way. If the before is portrait, take the after in portrait too, so the pair lines up.
  • Leave people and personal items out. Don’t photograph mail, documents, family photos or screens.
  • Check what the photo file carries. Phone cameras can store your location in each photo. Turn that off, or use a tool that removes it, before sending photos to anyone.

How to write up issues

Describe what you saw, not who caused it. Short and factual reads as professional:

  • “Crack in the bathroom mirror, lower left corner. Noticed on arrival. Photo attached.”
  • “Two rolls of toilet paper left. Dish soap almost empty.”
  • “Phone charger left in bedroom 2, placed in the kitchen drawer.”
  • “Kitchen tap drips when closed.”

A report you make yourself is a record of your work, not certified proof. Dates and photos come from you and your phone. For deposit disputes or insurance claims, ask the client or property manager what they need.

Example: vacation rental turnover

For a host, the most useful parts are usually the issues and the restock list, so put them near the top:

  1. Header: property nickname (not the full address), date, check-out and next check-in time.
  2. Ready for next guest: yes / no.
  3. Issues and items left behind, with photos.
  4. Restock: what you replaced and what is running low.
  5. Room by room: checklist and one before/after pair each.
  6. Linen and towels: count changed.
